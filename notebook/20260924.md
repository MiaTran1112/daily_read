## Variance Explained: Understanding empirical Bayes estimation (using baseball statistics) (https://varianceexplained.org/r/empirical_bayes_baseball/)

- \(1/2\) does not mean the same thing as \(50/100\); nor does \(0/1\) mean the same thing as \(0/1000\).
- One approach is to filter out all cases that don’t meet some minimum, but this isn’t always an option: you’re throwing away useful information.

- Empirical Bayes estimation: the goal of improving our estimate of each player’s batting average
    - Step 1: Estimate a prior from all your data
        - we know we want to fit the following model: $$X\sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_0, \beta_0)$$
        - We just need to pick $\alpha_0$ and $\beta_0$, which we call “hyper-parameters” of our model. 
    - Step 2: Use that distribution as a prior for each individual estimate
        - it’s as simple as adding $\alpha_0$to the number of hits, and $\alpha_0$ + $\beta_0$ to the total number of at-bats.

        E.g: For example, consider our hypothetical batter from the introduction that went up 1000 times, and got 300 hits. We would estimate his batting average as:
        $$\frac{300+\alpha_0}{1000+\alpha_0+\beta_0}=\frac{300+78.7}{1000+78.7+224.9}=0.29$$

        How about the batter who went up only 10 times and got 4 hits? We would estimate his batting average as:
        $$\frac{4+\alpha_0}{10+\alpha_0+\beta_0}=\frac{4+78.7}{10+78.7+224.9}=0.264$$
