### Operator's Handbook: How to solve problems (https://www.operatorshandbook.com/p/how-to-solve-problems)

**"Very few people, however, are good at reliably solving problems they’re encountering for the first time."**

1. Why problem solving fails so often
- start discussing solutions before they’ve fully understood the problem.
    - moving too fast can actually end up taking more time than a structured, deliberate problem-solving approach.

2. Assessing the nature of the problem
- Is it a one-way door or two-way door decision?
    - **how hard (or costly) it is to reverse**
        - If it’s easy to reverse (two-way door) -> just go ahead and try a solution.
            - If wrong -> try another thing
        - If costly or impossible to reverse (one-way door):  **put in maximum effort to find the ideal solution.**
            - The cost of being wrong is high -> need to minimize the likelihood that you’re wrong by using a structured problem-solving approach
- Can you break the problem into smaller steps?
    - Is there an initial small test you can do to gather data and decide on next steps?
        -  splitting one big decision into a sequence of smaller ones -> make task simpler
- What are the opportunity costs of the decision?
    - deciding to do one thing means not being able to do something else
    - **Takeaway**: The more downstream implications the decision has, the more effort you should put into analyzing the problem and deciding on the path forward.
- Is it a recurring or one-off problem?
    - Scenario 1: If you are dealing with the SAME problem regularly
        -> Automate a solution
    - Scenario 2: If you deal with SIMILAR problems regularly
        -> Develop a **principle**
        -> gives you clear guidance on how to handle these things going forward
- Symptoms vs. root causes
    - ask yourself whether you’re actually solving the underlying problem, or just fighting the symptoms.

3. Defining the actual problem you’re trying to solve
- Without deeply understanding the problem, you can’t do a good job on the solution.
- Only once you have that context can you start being a thought partner in solving the problem. 
- **Problem <- Context <- Task**

4. Identifying and structuring your options
- **MECE: Mutually Exclusive and Collectively Exhaustive**:
    - This means you try to find all possible options and define them so that none of them overlap.
- First step: lay out all possible options.
- Second step: narrow things down
- So how do you come up with the possible options?
    - a brainstorming session 
    - a single person should be in charge of cleaning up and summarizing everything in a MECE way.
    -> avoid half-baked and overlapping ideas
    - **issue tree**

5. Evaluating the options
- Step 1: Developing an evaluation framework
    - figure out how you’re going to compare your options against each other so you can eventually frame up a decision.
- Step 2: Analyzing the options
    - analyze each option across the criteria you came up with.:
        - by data analysis or modeling or research or talking to subject matter experts 
    - spend time effectively:
        - Prioritization: spend most of your time on the factors that will affect the decision the most.
        - Sequencing: start with non-negotiable requirements
-> Goal: start with the analysis that can eliminate as many options as possible early on, so you can minimize the amount of work you have to do.

### Operator's Handbook: How to be "high agency" at work (https://www.operatorshandbook.com/p/how-to-be-high-agency-at-work)

#### A brief primer on agency
**(High) Agency is the belief that you can shape your circumstances and achieve your goals through your own actions, rather than waiting for ideal conditions — and the ability to follow through on this belief.**
-> You think you can make things happen, and then you find ways to make them happen.

#### Three core principles for increasing your agency
1. Focus on what you can control instead of making excuses

2. Assume you can do anything until proven otherwise
- Most people’s default is to assume they can’t do something until they figure out how to do it
-> This is one of the most common, and most severe, forms of self-sabotage.

3. Don’t wait for someone to tell you what to do or give you permission
-  If you want to get things done, it’s often best to keep the number of people involved to an absolute minimum, do the thing, and then get everyone else comfortable with the result.
- set a deadline for people to give feedback

4. Concrete ways to be “high agency” at work
- In career development:
    - Nobody cares about your career as much as you do; and if you don’t get what you need from your manager, it’s up to you to take initiative and change that.
- In how you approach work:
    - Get sign-off on your approach instead of asking what to do
        - **If you don’t do any work unless your manager tells you to do something, you’re essentially asking to be micromanaged.**
        - Tell your manager what you intend to do and give them an opportunity to provide input
    - Figure out how to make something work instead of finding reasons why it won’t
- In how you handle blockers:
    - Raising your bar for what constitutes a blocker: exhaust all of the options available to you first
    - Reduce your reliance on other teams
    - Anticipate blockers and bottlenecks
- By just doing things: 
    - If there’s no clear owner (and you need it done), do it yourself
    - Just do small tasks instead of delegating them

**Agency can be learned**
